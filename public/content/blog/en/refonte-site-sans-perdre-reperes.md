Redesigning a website involves more than changing its colours. Visitors may have saved pages, search engines already know some URLs, and your team depends on content that works. A useful redesign starts with an inventory.

## Identify what should stay

List the existing pages, their URLs, useful content and the enquiries they receive. Note outdated information, pages that no longer serve a purpose and pages that still answer customers' questions well.

Talk to the people who handle calls and messages. They often know which details are missing from the website and which pages customers mention.

## Prepare content before finalising layouts

The new navigation should reflect visitors' needs, not just the company structure. Draft the main messages, services, contact details and calls to action before locking in page layouts. You can then check that the design supports the content.

Decide who will keep the information current after launch. A more attractive site that is difficult to edit will quickly develop the same old errors.

## Handle changed addresses carefully

If URLs change, map each old page to the most relevant new one. Set up permanent redirects where possible and update internal links. Avoid sending every old page to the homepage: visitors would lose the answer they were looking for.

[Google recommends preparing this mapping and testing redirects](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes). Even when URLs stay the same, check titles, links and important pages after the redesign.

## Test before and after launch

On desktop and mobile, follow the main journeys: arrive on a service page, understand the offer and contact the business. Check links, images, contact details and readability. After launch, revisit old URLs and look for missing pages.

A useful redesign keeps the signposts that already help customers and fixes what slows them down. The new appearance is the visible result of deeper work on content and the customer journey.
