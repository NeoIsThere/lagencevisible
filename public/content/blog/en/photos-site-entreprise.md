Images give visitors a sense of your work before they read every word. They can show a place, a team or a tangible result. Their value depends less on quantity than on what they explain.

## Choose images that answer a question

For each photo, ask what visitors will learn. A workshop image can show where something is made; a project photo can illustrate a service; a portrait can help identify the person customers will speak to.

A generic image may decorate a page, but it does not show that your business carried out the work pictured. If you use illustrative images, do not present them as completed projects or members of your team.

## Check permissions before publishing

Make sure you have the right to use a photo and to show any identifiable people or places in that context. Obtain the permissions you need. If a client wants privacy, frame the photo differently or choose another example.

Keep a record of where each image came from. That makes future updates easier and helps you avoid keeping a photo whose usage rights are unclear.

## Support reading without slowing the page

Choose a sharp image suited to the space it fills. Avoid loading an enormous file for a small thumbnail. Check the result on a phone: automatic cropping can cut off the subject or make embedded text unreadable.

A short caption can provide context the image alone cannot: the type of project, your role or a detail worth noticing. Put important information in page text rather than baking it into a photo.

## Make the image understandable to everyone

An informative image needs alternative text that conveys its essential information. A purely decorative image can have empty alternative text. The right choice depends on what the image does, as explained in the [W3C images tutorial](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/).

The right photo helps people understand your business. A small, coherent selection with useful descriptions beats a large gallery without context.
